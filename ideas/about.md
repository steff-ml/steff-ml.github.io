My mission is to build the infrastructure that breaks down the data silos costing
organisations money and I came to it by an unusual route.

I work as a Databricks data engineer in the mechanical engineering industry. But
I'm not a data engineer who's curious about biology, or a career-switcher hoping
to pick up a new domain on the job. I have a PhD in biology and a decade inside
the life sciences. What I'm doing now isn't a leap into an unfamiliar field —
it's a return to my home domain, carrying engineering skills I didn't have the
first time around.

That combination is rare, and it's the whole point of this site.

## Who is this guy?

Before data engineering, I studied biophysics and synthetic biology, then did a
PhD in biology. During it, we published a paper on a molecular mechanism linking
metabolism to DNA replication, one of many findings showing that a cell's
metabolic state drives the behaviour of everything else it does. It taught me
something I've never stopped applying: **you cannot understand one process in
isolation. You have to understand the system.**

I've spent a decade learning to do that for cells. I now do it for industrial
processes with the data-engineering toolkit to actually build the systems that
make it possible.

The paper, for the curious: [Pyruvate kinase, a metabolic sensor powering
glycolysis, drives the metabolic control of DNA replication](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12915-022-01278-3),
*BMC Biology*.

## What does that have to do with money?

Data silos are everywhere in the (bio)sciences — for organisational reasons far
more than technical ones. Two examples.

**Rare disease research.** Data stays locked up to protect IP, and there's often
no regulatory-compliant way to exchange it. The result is redundant studies and
underpowered statistics, which raises the risk of a drug being rejected.[^1]
Regulatory rejection is extraordinarily expensive.

**Biomanufacturing.** Different departments run different systems that were never
designed to talk. Researchers track samples in a LIMS. Process engineers track
production in an MES. Finance and supply chain run an ERP. Quality runs a
QMS.[^2] Every manual data exchange is an opportunity for error, a delay in
operational decisions, and an hour a bioprocess engineer spends cleaning data
instead of interpreting it. Organisations that integrate these systems report
meaningfully fewer process deviations and faster batch release[^3] — but the
harder-to-measure cost is the analytical work that never happens because everyone
is reconciling spreadsheets.

Environments like these need people who are both capable technical data engineers
*and* fluent enough in the domain to talk to every one of those stakeholders and
work out what should actually be built.

That's the gap I want to work in.
